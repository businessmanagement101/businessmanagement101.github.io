### **Page 1: The Big Picture: Why We're Doing This**

**Title:** **Thinking Like a Strategist: An Introduction to Concept Mapping** 🗺️

**Hook:** How do successful leaders see connections that others miss? They don't just see a list of ideas; they see a system. A concept map is a tool for seeing that system. It helps you move from simply *knowing* business terms to *understanding* how they work together to create value.

**What is a Concept Map?**
A concept map is a visual diagram that shows the relationships between ideas. Think of it as a **road map for your thoughts**.
* It's not just a list of destinations (the **concepts**).
* It shows the highways and roads that connect them (the **relationships**).
* It helps you find the most insightful routes between different ideas.

**Why Use It for This Assignment?**
The goal of your assignment is to build your own **integrated theory of management**. A concept map is the perfect tool because it:
1.  **Forces You to Make Connections:** It pushes you to find the hidden links between topics from different weeks of the course (e.g., how does 'Organizational Culture' impact 'Supply Chain Efficiency'?).
2.  **Builds a Visual Outline:** Your finished map becomes the visual blueprint for your written "Theoretical Narrative," making the writing process much easier.
3.  **Focuses on Analysis, Not Just Description:** It helps you focus on the *relationship between theories*—the core requirement of the assignment.

### **Page 2: The Building Blocks: How to Create a Concept Map**

**Title: The Anatomy of a Great Concept Map** 🧠

A strong concept map has a few key components. Let's use a simple, non-business example: "Making a good breakfast."



1.  **Concepts:** These are the key nouns or ideas, usually enclosed in boxes or circles.
    * *Example:* `Breakfast`, `Coffee`, `Eggs`, `Heat`

2.  **Hierarchy:** Arrange concepts from the most general at the top to the most specific at the bottom.
    * *Example:* `Breakfast` is at the top, with `Coffee` and `Eggs` branching below it.

3.  **Linking Words:** These are short verbs or phrases written on the lines connecting concepts. **These are essential!** Without them, the map has no meaning.
    * *Example:* `is made with`, `requires`, `can be`

4.  **Propositions:** A proposition is a full idea made of two concepts joined by a linking phrase. This is the basic unit of meaning in your map.
    * *Example:* `[Coffee]` --- `requires` ---> `[Heat]`

5.  **Cross-Links:** This is where the deepest thinking happens! Cross-links are connections between different branches of your map.
    * *Example:* A cross-link could show that the `[Heat]` needed for coffee --- `comes from the` ---> `[Stove]`, which is also used to cook the `[Eggs]`.

### **Page 3: Guided Workshop: From Question to Map**

**Title: Workshop: Building a Map for Your Assignment**

Let's apply this directly to the assignment. Every great map starts with a great **Conceptual Focus Question**.

**Step 1: Start with the Anchor Question**
We'll use an example question from the assignment description:
> "How does 'Competitive Advantage' depend on the integration of 'Brand Equity' (Marketing) and 'Supply Chain Efficiency' (Operations)?"

**Step 2: Brainstorm a "Parking Lot" of Concepts**
Just list out all the related course concepts you can think of. Don't organize them yet.
* *Parking Lot:* Competitive Advantage, Brand Equity, Customer Loyalty, Supply Chain, Logistics, Cost Leadership, Differentiation, Quality Control, Marketing...

**Step 3: Build the Hierarchy (The Skeleton)**
Place your central concept at the top. Group related ideas into main branches.



**Step 4: Find the "Aha!" Moments with Cross-Links**
Now, connect the different branches. This is how you build your core argument. How does a concept in the "Operations" branch affect a concept in the "Marketing" branch?

* **Proposition 1:** `[Efficient Supply Chain]` --- `leads to` ---> `[Lower Costs]`
* **Proposition 2:** `[Lower Costs]` --- `enables` ---> `[Competitive Pricing]`
* **Cross-Link Proposition:** `[Competitive Pricing]` --- `can strengthen` ---> `[Brand Equity]`

**Step 5: Translate Your Map into Your Narrative**
Each meaningful path or cross-link on your map is a key point for your paper. The cross-link above becomes a powerful analytical sentence:

> "This analysis argues that operational tools like an **efficient supply chain** are not merely cost-saving measures but are, in fact, foundational to building **brand equity**. By enabling **lower costs** and more **competitive pricing**, operational excellence directly supports a marketing strategy aimed at building a loyal customer base, as seen with companies like Amazon or Walmart."

### **Page 4: Your Turn: Brainstorming Your Project**

**Title: Activity: Laying the Foundation for Your Assignment** ✏️

Now it's your turn to get started. Set a timer and work through these two steps.

**Part 1: Draft Your Focus Question (15 mins)**
* **Task:** Look through the course syllabus and pick two or three major concepts that you find interesting (e.g., Organizational Culture, Disruptive Innovation, Management Ideology, Porter's Five Forces).
* **Action:** Brainstorm 2-3 possible **conceptual focus questions** about the relationship between them. Use the examples in the assignment description as a guide.

**Part 2: Create a Concept "Parking Lot" (15 mins)**
* **Task:** Choose your strongest focus question from Part 1.
* **Action:** On a blank piece of paper or a document, create a "parking lot" by listing at least 10-15 course concepts that might be relevant to answering your question. Don't worry about the structure yet—just get the ideas out!

You're right, developing a strong focus question is often the hardest part of this kind of analytical work. Evaluating different strategies is a great way to help students overcome this hurdle.

Here’s an evaluation of the approaches you suggested, along with a few other ideas to add to the mix.

### **1. Brainstorming with AI** 🤖

This is a modern and powerful approach for overcoming the initial "blank page" problem.

* **Pros:**
    * **Excellent for Idea Generation:** AI can quickly generate dozens of potential questions and connections a student might not have considered.
    * **Lowers the Barrier to Entry:** It's a low-pressure way to start exploring relationships between concepts without the fear of being "wrong."
    * **Helps with Phrasing:** AI can suggest more analytical wording, moving students from descriptive questions (e.g., "What is brand equity?") to analytical ones (e.g., "How does brand equity create a competitive moat?").

* **Cons:**
    * **Risk of Superficiality:** The AI lacks the specific context of your course lectures and readings. It might generate questions that are too generic or don't align with your key themes.
    * **Can Discourage Critical Thinking:** Students might be tempted to simply copy-paste a question without truly understanding or owning it.

* **Verdict:** Highly recommended as a **starting point**. It's a fantastic tool for brainstorming, but students must be coached to use the AI's output as raw material that they then **refine** and connect to specific course concepts.

---

### **2. The Placeholder Question Method** 🏗️

This is a classic, process-oriented strategy that encourages emergent, bottom-up thinking.

* **Pros:**
    * **Ensures Grounding in Course Material:** Because the question is finalized *after* brainstorming concepts, it's guaranteed to be relevant to the course content the student actually knows and finds interesting.
    * **Reduces Initial Pressure:** It allows students to dive into the material they find engaging without worrying about having the "perfect" question from the outset.
    * **Builds a More Authentic Argument:** The final question emerges organically from the student’s own identified connections, often leading to a more insightful and well-supported narrative.

* **Cons:**
    * **Can Feel Unstructured:** Some students may feel anxious without a clear question guiding their initial brainstorming. They might create a "parking lot" of concepts that is too scattered.
    * **Requires a Strong Final Step:** Students must be disciplined in that final step to sharpen the placeholder ("My question is about culture and profit") into a truly **analytical** question ("To what extent does a stakeholder-focused culture create predictable tensions with a short-term shareholder profit motive?").

* **Verdict:** An excellent and highly effective method, especially for students who learn by doing. It frames the task as one of **discovery** rather than proving a preconceived notion.

### **Additional Ideas to Help Students**

Here are two more structured approaches you can offer:

### **3. The "Find the Friction" Method** 🔥

Business and management are full of inherent tensions and trade-offs. These are a goldmine for powerful conceptual questions.

* **How it Works:** Encourage students to look for concepts in the course that seem to pull in opposite directions. The focus question then explores the "why" and "how" behind that conflict.
* **Example Brainstorming Prompts:**
    * What's the tension between **short-term profitability** and **long-term brand building**?
    * How does a culture of **standardization/efficiency** conflict with the need for **radical innovation**?
    * In what ways does a **shareholder-centric ideology** create challenges for authentic **corporate social responsibility**?
* **Why it Works:** This method naturally leads to analytical questions because it's built around a central problem or debate. It moves students away from simply connecting concepts and toward analyzing their dynamic interplay.

### **4. The "Concept Mash-up" Method** 🧩

This is a simple, creative exercise to force connections between seemingly unrelated topics.

* **How it Works:**
    1.  Have students create two lists of major concepts from the course (e.g., List A: Strategy & Finance, List B: Culture & Marketing).
    2.  Ask them to draw a line connecting one concept from List A to one from List B.
    3.  Their task is to formulate a "how" or "why" question about the relationship between the two.
* **Example:**
    * A student connects **`Porter's Five Forces`** (List A) with **`Organizational Culture`** (List B).
    * **The resulting question:** "How can a company's internal **Organizational Culture** serve as a primary defense against pressures identified in **Porter's Five Forces**?"
* **Why it Works:** This structured brainstorming can spark highly original and insightful questions by forcing students to think across the typical silos of business functions.

### **Evaluation of the Idea**

This is a significant improvement on the original outline for several key reasons:

* **Pros:**
    * **Reduces Cognitive Load:** Trying to learn how to create a focus question *and* build a map at the same time can be overwhelming. Dedicating a workshop solely to the focus question allows students to concentrate on this critical first step.
    * **Provides a Shared Context:** Using a single video for the entire class is brilliant. It ensures everyone is working from the same set of facts and concepts, making peer discussions and instructor examples much more effective.
    * **Promotes Active Learning:** Watching a video with a specific goal (to find the concepts and draft a question) is a fantastic active learning exercise. It’s more engaging and memorable than a lecture.
    * **Creates a Concrete Example:** It grounds the abstract task of concept mapping in a tangible case study. Students will see the entire process—from question to map—applied to one consistent example before they have to do it for their own, more abstract assignment.

* **Cons:**
    * **Video Selection is Critical:** The success of this entire sequence hinges on your choice of video. It must be short (5-10 minutes), engaging, and conceptually dense enough to support a good map. (e.g., a Vox *Explained* episode on a business topic, a short TED Talk, or a mini-documentary about a company's strategy).
    * **Time Management:** This approach will likely take more time than the single workshop. You'll need to plan the timing carefully to fit it within your 90-minute session.

**Verdict:** This is a superior approach. The benefits of scaffolding the learning process and using a shared, concrete example far outweigh the potential downsides.
