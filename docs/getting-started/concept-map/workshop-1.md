# Workshop: Part I

## A Quick Recap

**How the Parts Work Together**

Before we dive into our hands-on practice, let's quickly recap how the building blocks of a concept map work together to create a powerful argument. A great way to understand this is by looking at it from two different points of view: the **Mapper** (the creator) and the **Reader** (the audience).

### **Mapper's Perspective**

When you are building a map, you will likely follow these steps:

1.  **Start with your Focus Question.** This is your North Star. It sets the boundaries for your map and gives you a clear goal. Everything you add should help you answer this question.
2.  **Establish your Concepts and Hierarchy.** You'll brainstorm key ideas and arrange them from the most general at the top to the most specific at the bottom. This creates the basic "skeleton" of your argument.
3.  **Form Propositions.** Using linking words, you connect your concepts to form meaningful sentences. This is the core act of sense-making, where you define the relationships and build the logic of your map.
4.  **Discover Cross-Links.** This is often the most exciting part! As you build your map, you'll start to see surprising connections between different branches. These "aha!" moments are where your deepest insights will come from.

### **Reader's Perspective**

When someone looks at your finished map, they will experience it in this way:

1.  **See the Focus Question.** The question makes a promise to the reader, telling them what argument to expect and what the map will explain.
2.  **Understand the Hierarchy.** The reader will quickly grasp your main topics and supporting points by following the structure from top to bottom. It's the primary path through your argument.
3.  **Read the Propositions.** The reader follows your propositions from concept to concept to understand the specific claims you are making. If your linking words are clear, your logic will be easy to follow.
4.  **Appreciate the Cross-Links.** For the reader, your cross-links are the most impressive part. They demonstrate sophisticated, big-picture thinking and reveal the systemic connections that make your argument truly insightful.


With this framework in mind, let's put it into practice.

### Before You Watch
**Setting the Context**

This video will serve as the shared case study for different parts ofthis workshop. The video is a powerful critique of one of the most influential ideas in modern business: **the belief that the primary purpose of a corporation is to "maximize shareholder value."**

Your goal is not to passively absorb this video but to actively analyze the speaker's argument. He is building a case, piece by piece, against this dominant ideology. Your task is to map out that case. As you watch, think like a consultant: What is the core problem the speaker has identified? What evidence does he use? And what are the consequences of the current system?

**Key Concepts to Listen For:** As you watch, keep an ear out for these key business terms. Jot down a quick definition or note when you hear them.

* Market Fundamentalism
* Maximizing Shareholder Value (MSV)
* Resource Allocation
* Financialization
* Stock Buybacks
* Stakeholders vs. Shareholders
* Worker Investment vs. Shareholder Risk


### As You Watch
**Guiding Questions**

Use these questions to guide your note-taking. I recommend pausing the video periodically to jot down your thoughts.

**Part 1: The Foundation (Timestamp 0:00 – 3:45)**

* The speaker begins by talking about how nations historically got rich. What does he say is the key driver of prosperity at the company level?
* What has been the traditional role of the state (government) in the U.S. economy, and how has that perception changed in recent decades according to the speaker?

**Part 2: The Core Ideology (Timestamp 3:45 – 7:50)**

* In your own words, what is the "Maximizing Shareholder Value" (MSV) ideology? What does it claim is the primary goal of a company?
* The MSV ideology claims that shareholders are the only ones who take a real financial risk. Who are the **two other groups** the speaker argues are also making risky, long-term investments in a company's success?

**Part 3: The Consequences (Timestamp 7:50 – 14:15)**

* What is a **stock buyback**? Why does the speaker argue it's a form of "legalized manipulation" of stock prices?
* According to the speaker, how does the focus on stock price and buybacks affect a company's ability to invest in its future (e.g., in its workers and in innovation)?
* How are top executive salaries connected to this system?


### After You Watch
**Synthesis & Reflection**

Take a few minutes to reflect on the video as a whole. Your answers to these questions will be the direct starting point for our in-class workshop.

1. **Find the Friction:** What is the central **tension** or **conflict** at the heart of the speaker's argument? (For example, "The tension between...")
1. **Draft a Focus Question:** Based on that tension, draft one or two potential **conceptual focus questions** that this video could help answer. Remember, the question should be about the relationship between the *ideas*.
    * *Example:* "How does a corporate focus on **'Short-Term Financial Metrics'** create a structural conflict with the goal of fostering long-term **'Employee Investment and Innovation'**?"
