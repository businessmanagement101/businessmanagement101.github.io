## **Course Description and Learning Objectives:**

Business organizations touch our lives in a variety of ways: we use their products and services, we accept them as our employers, we invest our money in them, we let them operate in our communities, and we let them influence policies and legislation at the state and federal level. Many critical life decisions such as choosing a job offer, making a financial investment, moving to a city, and fulfilling your civic duties can benefit from having a fundamental understanding of business, its management, and related contemporary issues that have broader implications for society. Helping students gain this understanding is the primary objective of this course.

This course has three major parts: theoretical foundations, business functions, and experiential learning. In the first part, we discuss the role of product and factor markets, impact of leadership and governance, dominant contemporary management ideology and emerging alternatives, and organizational design and development. In the second part, we discuss major business functions: finance, marketing, operations, and technology management. In the final part, we apply the concepts learned to analyze and understand a major business corporation and its managerial decisions. We use a learning tool called concept map to identify and connect key ideas from the three parts.

By the end of this course, you should be able to:

* Enumerate, describe, and analyze major business function decisions.  
* Analyze and explain strategic management decisions of a company and their potential outcomes.  
* Understand, evaluate, and apply foundational concepts that underlie contemporary management ideology.

### Overall Assessment & High-Level Recommendations

Your current structure is logical, moving from the external environment ("Foundations") to the internal workings of the firm ("Business Functions"). The primary opportunity for improvement lies in creating a more explicit bridge between these two modules and elevating some critical, underlying concepts.

1.  **Introduce a "Strategy" Session:** The most significant missing piece is a dedicated session on **Strategy**. You discuss elements of strategy everywhere—creating market failure, choosing which opportunities to pursue, product-market choices—but it's never formalized. A dedicated strategy session would act as the central nervous system of the course, connecting the *why* (Foundations) with the *how* (Business Functions).

2.  **Elevate "Operations Management":** The content listed for Sessions 6 & 7 is excellent macro-organizational behavior (OB) and organizational design (OD), but it doesn't cover the core of **Operations Management** (e.g., supply chains, quality, process management). Given its importance, Operations deserves to be treated as a full-fledged business function alongside Finance and Marketing.

3.  **Create a "People & Talent Management" Session:** The point about personality profiles and staffing is a seed for a larger, more crucial topic: Human Resource Management or People/Talent Management. For students whose primary interest is their own career, understanding how organizations attract, develop, and retain talent is invaluable. This is a core business function.

4.  **Refine the Thematic Flow:** We can strengthen the course's narrative arc. A powerful flow would be:
    * **The Playing Field:** What is the external world of business like? (Markets, Ideology)
    * **The Game Plan:** How does a firm decide how to compete and win in that world? (**Strategy**)
    * **The Engine:** How does a firm build the internal machine to execute that plan? (Organization, **Operations**, Culture)
    * **The Levers:** What functional tools does the firm use to operate the machine? (Finance, Marketing, People, Technology)

This revised flow makes the connection between each topic more explicit and causal.

#### **Module 2: Foundations - The Playing Field & The Game Plan**

**Session 3: The Playing Field: Markets, Value, and Failure**
*(This session remains largely as you planned, but with a sharpened focus on setting the stage for strategy.)*

* **Topics:**
    * Product markets and factor markets: The two arenas where firms operate.
    * The Spectrum of Competition: From perfect competition (commodities, stocks) to monopolistic competition (differentiated goods like cars, art, high-skilled labor).
    * **Central Concept: Economic Surplus.** Introduce this early. Businesses exist to create value for customers that exceeds the cost of their inputs. Profit is the portion of that surplus the firm captures.
    * Market Failure (The Economist's View): Externalities, information asymmetry (linking to used cars/Akerlof's "Market for Lemons"), public goods. This is market failure as a societal problem.
    * **Transition Question:** If perfect markets are "efficient," why is the goal of most businesses to escape them? This sets up the next session on Strategy.

**Session 4: The Game Plan: Strategy & Competitive Advantage**
*(This is a new, crucial session that acts as the bridge.)*

* **Topics:**
    * **What is Strategy?** The art of positioning an organization to create and sustain a competitive advantage. It’s the integrated set of choices that makes a company unique.
    * **Creating Market Failure (The Strategist's View):** This is where you introduce your brilliant framing. Strategy is the deliberate effort to create "market imperfections" that favor the firm.
        * Branding creates information asymmetry and customer loyalty.
        * Patents create a legal monopoly.
        * Proprietary processes or unique culture create cost advantages that are hard to copy.
    * **Foundational Strategic Frameworks (Simplified):**
        * **Porter's Five Forces:** A tool to analyze the "playing field" (industry structure) and its inherent profitability.
        * **SWOT Analysis:** A simple but effective tool for students to apply to their project company.
        * **Resource-Based View (VRIO Framework):** Why are some companies in the same industry more profitable than others? It depends on their internal **V**aluable, **R**are, **I**nimitable **O**rganizational capabilities. This elegantly connects the external view to the internal view.

**Session 5 & 6: The Captains: Leadership, Governance, and Ideology**
*(This combines and logically places your Sessions 4 & 5. The discussion of CEO and Board roles makes more sense *after* establishing that their primary job is to set and execute strategy.)*

* **Topics:**
    * The CEO's Role: Bridge between the external environment (markets, strategy) and the internal organization (resources, capabilities).
    * The Board of Directors: The oversight mechanism. Discuss their duties of care and loyalty.
    * **The Core Tension: Shareholder vs. Stakeholder Primacy.**
        * **Shareholder Argument:** Ground in agency theory, residual claimancy, and the role of capital markets in societal resource allocation. Your historical points about the Age of Exploration are perfect here.
        * **Stakeholder Argument:** Ground in externalities (environmental costs, social impact), the idea of the firm as a social entity, and the fact that legal protections for other stakeholders are often imperfect.
    * **Connecting to Strategy:** How does a company's chosen ideology (shareholder vs. stakeholder) shape its strategic goals (e.g., maximizing profit vs. balancing profit with social/environmental goals)? This becomes a real, tangible choice, not just a philosophical debate.

### How this Restructuring Benefits the Experiential Learning Module

This revised flow provides a crystal-clear roadmap for the students' final project. They can structure their company analysis in the exact same sequence as the course:

1.  **The Playing Field:** What industry is the company in? What are the market dynamics (Porter's Five Forces)? What is the dominant ideology in their sector?
2.  **The Game Plan:** What is the company's stated strategy? How are they trying to create a "market failure" or competitive advantage (VRIO)?

Yes, absolutely. The quality of a prompt is directly proportional to the quality and specificity of the information you provide. A powerful template isn't just a set of instructions; it's a structured container for the key variables of your pedagogical design.

Here are the details you should prepare for each session before using the template. Think of these as the "ingredients" you'll plug into the prompt "recipe."

### **Details to Prepare Before Each Prompting Session**

1.  **Specific Learning Objectives:** For each session, define precisely what a student should be able to *do* after reading the content. Use action verbs.

      * *Instead of:* "Students will learn about Porter's Five Forces."
      * *Use:* "After this session, students will be able to: 1) List and define each of Porter's Five Forces. 2) Analyze the attractiveness of a given industry using the framework. 3) Argue how a specific company's strategy is designed to mitigate one of the five forces."

2.  **Core Concepts & Key Vocabulary:** List the non-negotiable theories, frameworks, and terms that *must* be included and defined. This ensures the intellectual backbone of the session is present.

      * *Example for Strategy Session:* "Resource-Based View, VRIO Framework, Competitive Advantage, SWOT Analysis, Porter's Five Forces."

3.  **Narrative Hooks & Case Studies:** This is crucial for the "long-form article" style. Suggest 1-3 specific companies, historical events, or contemporary news stories that can be woven into the articles to illustrate the concepts.

      * *Example for Governance Session:* "The Dutch East India Company (history of shares), the 2008 financial crisis (board oversight failure), the rise of Patagonia (stakeholder model)."

4.  **Essential Tensions & Contrasting Perspectives:** To achieve the richness of *The Atlantic*, you need to explicitly ask for debate and nuance. What are the core arguments you want to present?

      * *Example for Ideology Session:* "The core tension is Milton Friedman's shareholder primacy vs. Edward Freeman's stakeholder theory. Please represent both arguments charitably before exploring a synthesis."

5.  **Connections to Other Sessions:** Briefly state how this session connects to previous or future topics. This helps the AI build a cohesive narrative across the course.

      * *Example for Marketing Session:* "Connect the concept of 'brand as a signal' back to the discussion of 'information asymmetry' from the Markets session. Frame marketing investment as a way to support the 'differentiation strategy' discussed in the Strategy session."

By preparing these five elements for each session, you provide the AI with the specific, high-level intellectual direction it needs. The template then becomes the scaffolding that ensures this direction is executed in the correct format and style every time.
